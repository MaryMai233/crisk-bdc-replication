\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
I replicate the baseline CRISK framework for ten large U.S. banks and extend its portfolio validation to business development companies (BDCs). The bank replication recovers the 2020 rise in climate beta and 85--87 percent of the published top-four CRISK magnitudes. For BDCs, a continuous portfolio climate-beta measure is positively related to traded climate beta, but inference is weaker than in the original Y-14 exercise. The gap is consistent with much coarser public portfolio disclosure and a shorter post-2020 sample. A broader post-KOL basket improves factor tracking but not small-cluster inference. Under a 50-percent climate-factor stress, mean BDC statutory asset-coverage buffer falls by 5.85 percentage points without a breach.

\vspace{0.14in}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} climate risk; CRISK; business development companies; private credit
\end{minipage}

\section{Introduction}

Jung, Engle, and Berner (2025) use a market-based climate factor to estimate time-varying bank climate beta and CRISK, then validate the beta with granular Y-14 loan portfolios. This paper asks whether the same market-to-portfolio mapping appears in publicly traded private credit.

The answer is broadly yes, but less precisely. The bank replication is close to the published benchmark. For BDCs, the portfolio signal strengthens when exposure is measured continuously rather than with a coarse brown-share proxy, while small-cluster inference remains weaker. The extension therefore highlights a difference in \emph{measurement and transmission}, not the absence of climate exposure.

\section{Data and Method}

The bank replication uses ten large U.S. banks. The BDC panel contains 19 firms and 380 firm-quarters from 2021Q1 to 2025Q4. BDC portfolio industries are taken from SEC Schedules of Investments and mapped to FF49 industries; the mapping covers 95.2 percent of reported portfolio weight overall. By comparison, the original Y-14 validation uses about five million loans and 666 bank-quarters, so the BDC exposure measure is necessarily much coarser \citep{JungEngleBerner2025}.

The original paper constructs four climate transition factors. This replication uses its headline \textbf{stranded-asset factor} only:
\begin{equation}
CF_t=0.70R_{Coal,t}+0.30R_{XLE,t}-R_{SPY,t}.
\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
After KOL liquidates, the strict replication uses the published top-five continuation. A pre-specified 15-security basket covering 77.1 percent of the September 2020 N-PORT common-stock schedule is used only as a robustness check \citep{VanEckNPORT2020}.

Institution and industry climate betas are estimated with the same GJR-GARCH/DCC framework as the replication archive. For a 50-percent climate-factor decline,
\begin{equation}
\LRMES_{i,t}=1-\exp\left[\beta^C_{i,t}\log(0.5)\right],
\qquad
\CRISK_{i,t}=0.08D_{i,t}-0.92(1-\LRMES_{i,t})E_{i,t}.
\label{eq:crisk}
\end{equation}
For BDCs, the bank capital ratio is not treated as a regulatory requirement. Capacity is evaluated using statutory asset coverage:
\begin{equation}
AC^{stress}_{i,q}=AC_{i,q}-100\left(\frac{E_{i,q}\LRMES_{i,q}}{D_{i,q}}\right).
\label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Bank replication}

The replication recovers the main bank result. Mean climate beta rises from 0.193 in 2019 to 0.424 in 2020; the cross-bank paired change is 0.230***. End-2020 top-four $m$CRISK is \$221.7 billion versus \$260.0 billion in the paper, and the 2020 CRISK increase is \$372.8 billion versus \$430.89 billion. \tabref{tab:bank} and \figref{fig:beta} summarize the result.

The remaining precision gap is mainly a measurement issue. Under one stricter BDC-and-quarter-fixed-effects specification, the standardized coefficient moves from $-0.032$ for a coarse brown share to 0.100 for FF12, 0.167* for rolling-OLS FF49, and 0.222** for DCC--FF49. \figref{fig:resolution} shows this gradient. With the strict top-five KOL continuation, the controlled DCC--FF49 equity coefficient is 0.402** and the 19-cluster wild-bootstrap $p$-value is 0.092. Expanding KOL to the 15-security 75-percent basket raises daily tracking from 0.454 to 0.790 but does not improve inference (wild $p=0.162$). Thus basket breadth is not the main reason BDC results are weaker than the Y-14 bank results.

The economic difference is also intuitive. The original bank mechanism runs from borrower climate exposure to loan default risk and then to bank equity, with relationship lending and specialization slowing adjustment. BDC equity additionally reflects NAV discounts, funding costs, fees, portfolio turnover, and manager choices. The same underlying credit exposure therefore maps less cleanly into traded equity beta.

\subsection{BDC statutory capacity}

Under the maintained 50-percent climate-factor stress, mean reported asset coverage falls from 203.28 to 197.43 percent. The mean buffer falls by 5.85 percentage points, and no observation breaches its applicable statutory threshold. The result is economically meaningful but not a claim that BDCs face the bank's 8-percent prudential capital rule.

\section{Conclusion}

The baseline CRISK replication is close to the published bank benchmark. In BDCs, continuous portfolio climate beta is informative about traded climate beta, but the mapping is less precise because public portfolio data are coarser and the sample begins after the key 2019--2020 repricing. A broader post-KOL basket improves factor tracking but not inference. The extension is therefore best read as evidence that the climate-beta framework is portable, while the transmission from underlying credit exposure to equity and regulatory capacity differs outside large banks. Code and detailed robustness outputs are available at \url{https://github.com/MaryMai233/crisk-bdc-replication}.
